First: most of what you'll google is now wrong

FedRAMP rebuilt its rules in mid-2026. The Consolidated Rules for 2026 went live on June 25, 2026, capping a modernization effort called FedRAMP 20x that GSA kicked off in March 2025. Three things you'll still read everywhere that are no longer true:

“You need an agency sponsor”

No longer required. FedRAMP now reviews and certifies directly — 20x has no sponsor requirement, and even the legacy path gained sponsor-free certifications in February 2026.

“The JAB will review your package”

The Joint Authorization Board no longer exists. The FedRAMP Authorization Act and OMB M-24-15 replaced it with a FedRAMP Board and a program office at GSA.

“Budget 12–18 months for review”

GSA reports the review timeline dropped from over a year to about five weeks, with the backlog eliminated in 2025. (Your own preparation time is still yours to spend.)

That's why this page leans on FedRAMP's machine-readable sources — the same data that powers the official Marketplace and the rules themselves, published as JSON — so the numbers here stay current even when the prose around them ages.

~1 minstep 2 of 6

What FedRAMP is, in three sentences

FedRAMP (the Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program) is the U.S. government's single security-approval program for cloud services, written into law at 44 U.S.C. §§ 3607–3616 and run out of GSA. The deal: a cloud provider proves its security once, and every federal agency can then reuse that approval instead of re-assessing from scratch — the law even gives reuse a formal “presumption of adequacy”. The public record of who's approved, in process, and reusable lives on the FedRAMP Marketplace — which is what the live section below draws from.

Why it matters to you: if you want a federal agency to use your cloud product, FedRAMP isn't a nice-to-have — for federal data in cloud services it is the required on-ramp, and agencies check the Marketplace before they talk to you.

Who's who (five parties, one goal)

You — the CSP

Cloud Service Provider

Build and operate the service, implement the security requirements, produce the (now machine-readable) evidence.

The assessor — 3PAO

Third-Party Assessment Organization

An accredited independent firm that tests your claims before FedRAMP sees them.

FedRAMP (PMO at GSA)

Sets the standards, runs the reviews and the Marketplace — your primary counterpart under the 2026 rules.

The FedRAMP Board

Governance and policy oversight (the JAB's replacement — it does not review individual packages).

Agencies — your customers

Consume your service and reuse your approval; under the legacy path one of them issued your first Authority to Operate (ATO).

Checkpoint — your package is submitted today. Who reviews it?

Under the 2026 rules FedRAMP reviews and certifies directly; the JAB was dissolved by M-24-15, and sponsor-free certification now covers both 20x and the legacy path.

The fork in the road: legacy Rev5 vs. FedRAMP 20x

Two systems are running side by side while the program transitions — and the clock on the old one is public. If you're starting fresh, you are almost certainly choosing between them right now, which is why the difference is worth two of your ten minutes.

What does it cost? Honest answer: there are no official figures — FedRAMP considered requiring assessment-cost reporting and decided against it (RFC-0019, “Not Implemented”). Industry estimates for a traditional Rev5 effort commonly land anywhere from the mid six figures to a few million dollars all-in — treat those as vendor marketing, not gospel. 20x is expected to cost less mostly because it automates evidence; it's too new for reliable numbers.

Checkpoint — it's mid-2027 and you're just starting. Which path can you take?

The published schedule closes Rev5 to new certifications on June 11, 2027; 20x has been the generally available path since the 2026 rules launched.

Certification Classes: A, B, C, D

The 2026 rules organize certifications into classes of increasing assurance — “minimal assurance at Class A to significant assurance at Class D”. They loosely echo the old Low/Moderate/High impact levels, but the official definitions are what count. A rough, honest guide:

Which sounds most like your service? (a study aid — the official class definitions decide, not this page)

Look at Class A

FedRAMP describes Class A as the entry tier for organizations with mature security programs that are new to the federal marketplace — minimal assurance, fastest on-ramp. Its pipeline opened August 3, 2026.

Look at Class B

Aimed at small-scale, light-use services (the territory the old Low baseline covered). Available via 20x — pipeline opens August 31, 2026 — and via Rev5 until it sunsets.

Look at Class C

The workhorse tier for common enterprise services (roughly the old Moderate territory, where most of the marketplace lives — see the live data below). Available via 20x from August 31, 2026, and via Rev5 until sunset.

Class D — not in 20x yet

The highest-assurance class is still in development on the 20x side (planned for a future phase), so today high-baseline work means the Rev5 path while it remains open.

Key Security Indicators — the heart of 20x

A KSI flips the old model. Instead of writing a narrative explaining how you plan to be secure, each indicator is a concrete security outcome you demonstrate — with largely automated, machine-readable evidence, validated continuously rather than re-audited yearly.
